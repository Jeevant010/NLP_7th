### Question 1: Bayesian Spelling Correction
In the Noisy Channel Model for spelling correction, we seek $\hat{c} = \arg\max_c P(c \mid w) = \arg\max_c \frac{P(w \mid c) P(c)}{P(w)}$. Why can the denominator $P(w)$ be safely omitted in computation?

- [ ] (A) Because $P(w)$ evaluates to 1 in normalized error models.
- [ ] (B) Because $P(w)$ is independent of candidate $c$ and represents a constant positive denominator across all candidates for a given typo $w$.
- [ ] (C) Because $P(w)$ depends entirely on the language model prior rather than the observed text.
- [ ] (D) Because Bayes' theorem only applies to the numerator in discrete distributions.

<details>
<summary><b>View Answer & Trap Analysis</b></summary>

* **Correct Answer**: **(B)**
* **Why it's correct**: The observed misspelled string $w$ is fixed. Its marginal probability $P(w) = \sum_{c'} P(w \mid c') P(c')$ is a constant strictly positive value for all candidate words $c$. Dividing candidates by a constant does not alter the $\arg\max$.
* **The Trap in (A) & (C)**: (A) falsely claims $P(w) = 1$, which is incorrect (it is typically very small). (C) confuses the prior $P(c)$ with the marginal evidence $P(w)$.
</details>

### Question 2: Perplexity Boundary Tokens
When calculating the perplexity of a test corpus, why must the end-of-sentence token ($\langle /s \rangle$) be included in the total token count $N$, while the start-of-sentence token ($\langle s \rangle$) is excluded?

- [ ] (A) Because $\langle s \rangle$ has probability zero under MLE.
- [ ] (B) Because $\langle /s \rangle$ is an actual predicted event (terminal token) generated by the language model, whereas $\langle s \rangle$ serves solely as conditioning context.
- [ ] (C) Because including $\langle s \rangle$ would make perplexity undefined or negative.
- [ ] (D) Because smoothing algorithms only discount right-side boundary elements.

<details>
<summary><b>View Answer & Trap Analysis</b></summary>

* **Correct Answer**: **(B)**
* **Why it's correct**: The language model explicitly predicts when a sentence terminates: $P(\langle /s \rangle \mid w_n)$. Thus, $\langle /s \rangle$ is an event in the probability space. $\langle s \rangle$ is never predicted; it only conditions the first word $P(w_1 \mid \langle s \rangle)$.
* **The Trap in (A) & (C)**: Distractors invent mathematical anomalies (zero or negative perplexity) that have no theoretical basis.
</details>

### Question 3: TF-IDF Document Discriminability
Suppose a corpus has $N = 2$ documents: $D_1 =$ `"dog bites man"` and $D_2 =$ `"man feeds dog"`. Using $IDF(t) = \ln(N / DF(t))$ and raw term frequency, what is the Cosine Similarity between the TF-IDF vectors of $D_1$ and $D_2$?

- [ ] (A) $0.667$
- [ ] (B) $0.500$
- [ ] (C) $0.000$
- [ ] (D) $1.000$

<details>
<summary><b>View Answer & Trap Analysis</b></summary>

* **Correct Answer**: **(C) 0.000**
* **Step-by-step Math**:
  * Shared words: `"dog"` and `"man"` appear in both documents ($DF = 2$).
  * $IDF(\text{"dog"}) = \ln(2/2) = \ln(1) = 0$.
  * $IDF(\text{"man"}) = \ln(2/2) = \ln(1) = 0$.
  * Unique words: `"bites"` (only in $D_1$, $IDF = \ln 2$) and `"feeds"` (only in $D_2$, $IDF = \ln 2$).
  * $\mathbf{v}_1 = [\ln 2, 0, 0, 0]$ (on `"bites"`) and $\mathbf{v}_2 = [0, 0, \ln 2, 0]$ (on `"feeds"`).
  * Dot product $\mathbf{v}_1 \cdot \mathbf{v}_2 = 0 \implies \text{Cosine Similarity} = 0$.
* **The Trap in (A)**: Many students count word overlap: $\frac{2 \text{ shared words}}{3 \text{ words}} = 0.667$. That is Jaccard/bag-of-words similarity, NOT TF-IDF cosine similarity!
</details>

### Question 4: FastText Out-Of-Vocabulary Superiority
Why is FastText capable of generating meaningful vector representations for Out-Of-Vocabulary (OOV) words, whereas standard Word2Vec and GloVe cannot?

- [ ] (A) FastText trains using character-level Transformers.
- [ ] (B) FastText represents each word as the sum of its character $n$-gram embeddings, allowing unseen words to be composed from seen subwords.
- [ ] (C) FastText automatically queries WordNet synsets for unseen words.
- [ ] (D) FastText dynamically updates the embedding matrix using online gradient descent during inference.

<details>
<summary><b>View Answer & Trap Analysis</b></summary>

* **Correct Answer**: **(B)**
* **Why it's correct**: FastText breaks tokens into character $n$-grams (e.g. `un`, `break`, `able`). An unseen compound word at test time combines pre-trained subword embeddings to produce an informative embedding vector.
</details>

### Question 5: Transformer Attention Scaling Factor
In Transformer scaled dot-product attention $\text{softmax}\left(\frac{QK^\top}{\sqrt{d_k}}\right)V$, why is the dot-product divided by $\sqrt{d_k}$?

- [ ] (A) To convert the dot-products into Euclidean distances.
- [ ] (B) To prevent large values of $d_k$ from causing dot-products to explode, which pushes softmax into regions with vanishing gradients.
- [ ] (C) To ensure the output vector norm equals 1.
- [ ] (D) To eliminate the need for multi-head projections.

<details>
<summary><b>View Answer & Trap Analysis</b></summary>

* **Correct Answer**: **(B)**
* **Why it's correct**: If $q$ and $k$ are independent random variables with mean 0 and variance 1, their dot product has mean 0 and variance $d_k$. For large $d_k$, logits become very large, causing softmax outputs to saturate near 0 or 1, where gradients are virtually zero.
</details>

### Question 6: BERT vs GPT Attention Masking
What is the core difference between the attention masks used in BERT and GPT during training?

- [ ] (A) BERT masks 100% of tokens; GPT masks 15% of tokens.
- [ ] (B) BERT uses a bidirectional attention mask allowing tokens to attend to both left and right context, whereas GPT uses a lower-triangular causal mask preventing attention to future tokens.
- [ ] (C) BERT uses static attention masks; GPT uses learned attention weights without masking.
- [ ] (D) BERT applies attention only across adjacent words; GPT applies attention across the full document.

<details>
<summary><b>View Answer & Trap Analysis</b></summary>

* **Correct Answer**: **(B)**
* **Why it's correct**: BERT is a Transformer Encoder designed for bidirectional contextual representation (via MLM). GPT is an autoregressive Transformer Decoder that enforces causality using a lower-triangular mask ($M_{ij} = -\infty$ for $j > i$).
</details>

### Question 7: BERT Train-Test Masking Discrepancy
Why does BERT use the **80/10/10 rule** (80% $[MASK]$, 10% random word, 10% unchanged) instead of replacing all 15% chosen tokens with $[MASK]$?

- [ ] (A) Because replacing all tokens with $[MASK]$ would cause cross-entropy loss to evaluate to negative infinity.
- [ ] (B) Because the $[MASK]$ token never appears during fine-tuning or real-world inference; the 80/10/10 rule mitigates this train-test mismatch by forcing the encoder to represent every token robustly.
- [ ] (C) Because WordPiece tokenizers cannot parse more than 80% mask tokens in a sequence.
- [ ] (D) To prevent the model from over-relying on Next Sentence Prediction.

<details>
<summary><b>View Answer & Trap Analysis</b></summary>

* **Correct Answer**: **(B)**
* **Why it's correct**: At downstream inference time (sentiment, QA), input text never has $[MASK]$ tokens. If BERT only learned to predict when seeing $[MASK]$, its contextual representations on natural text would be uncalibrated.
</details>

### Question 8: Yarowsky's Algorithm Heuristics
What are the two core linguistic heuristics that form the foundation of Yarowsky's bootstrapping algorithm for Word Sense Disambiguation?

- [ ] (A) One sense per syntax, and one sense per corpus.
- [ ] (B) One sense per collocation, and one sense per discourse.
- [ ] (C) One lemma per synset, and one hypernym per token.
- [ ] (D) Maximum marginal relevance, and minimal semantic drift.

<details>
<summary><b>View Answer & Trap Analysis</b></summary>

* **Correct Answer**: **(B)**
* **Why it's correct**:
  1. *One sense per collocation*: Local neighboring words provide strong, consistent clues about the target word's sense.
  2. *One sense per discourse*: In a single text/document, a polysemous word almost always retains the exact same sense throughout.
</details>

### Question 10: Pointwise Mutual Information (PMI) Bias
Why does standard Pointwise Mutual Information $\text{PMI}(w, c) = \log_2 \frac{P(w, c)}{P(w)P(c)}$ produce distorted, artificially high scores for very rare words?

- [ ] (A) Because rare words have high joint counts $C(w, c)$.
- [ ] (B) Because the product of the marginal probabilities $P(w)P(c)$ in the denominator is extremely small, blowing up the ratio.
- [ ] (C) Because the logarithm of small numbers approaches infinity.
- [ ] (D) Because rare words violate Zipf's Law.

<details>
<summary><b>View Answer & Trap Analysis</b></summary>

* **Correct Answer**: **(B)**
* **Why it's correct**: If $C(w) = 1$ and $C(c) = 1$, then $P(w) = 1/N$ and $P(c) = 1/N$. Their product is $1/N^2$. Even with a single co-occurrence $P(w, c) = 1/N$, the ratio evaluates to $\frac{1/N}{1/N^2} = N$, giving an enormous score $\log_2 N$.
</details>

### Question 11: Maximal Marginal Relevance (MMR)
In the MMR formula for multi-document summarisation:
$$\text{MMR} = \arg\max_{D_i \in R \setminus S} \left[ \lambda \cdot \text{Sim}_1(D_i, Q) - (1 - \lambda) \max_{D_j \in S} \text{Sim}_2(D_i, D_j) \right]$$
What happens when parameter $\lambda$ is set close to $1$?

- [ ] (A) The summary maximizes diversity and minimizes redundancy with zero regard for query relevance.
- [ ] (B) The selection focuses almost purely on query relevance, resulting in high redundancy among selected sentences.
- [ ] (C) The formula simplifies to random sentence sampling.
- [ ] (D) Sentences are selected purely on the basis of word length.

<details>
<summary><b>View Answer & Trap Analysis</b></summary>

* **Correct Answer**: **(B)**
* **Why it's correct**: The second term $(1 - \lambda)$ represents the redundancy penalty against already chosen sentences $S$. When $\lambda \to 1$, this penalty vanishes, leading to greedy selection of query-relevant sentences that repeat the exact same information.
</details>
